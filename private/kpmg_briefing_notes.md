# KPMG Strategy & Operations Case Competition 2026
## Oral Briefing Notes

> This document is a cleaned summary of oral comments made by KPMG representatives during the case briefing.
>
> It is not an official written case document.
> Where wording was unclear in the transcript, this is explicitly noted.
> The original raw transcript is preserved separately for verification.

---

# 1. Core objective

The task is to identify investment niches in Kazakhstan that could realistically attract foreign strategic investors.

The underlying logic is:

- an investor enters Kazakhstan;
- invests capital;
- establishes or develops production / business activity locally;
- serves a viable domestic and/or export market;
- earns an economically attractive return.

The KPMG representative emphasized that investor economics must come first:

> The niche must make economic sense for the investor.

Strategic relevance for Kazakhstan matters, but it cannot compensate for a fundamentally unattractive investor proposition.

---

# 2. Kazakhstan-specific opportunity logic

When assessing opportunities, teams should consider Kazakhstan's existing advantages, including where relevant:

- existing production capabilities;
- resource base;
- geographic position;
- access to large neighboring consumer markets;
- potential export demand;
- infrastructure;
- existing industrial capabilities;
- Kazakhstan's broader strategic economic agenda.

Geography should not be treated as a generic statement such as "Kazakhstan is between Europe and Asia."

It should translate into an economic advantage for a specific opportunity.

Example logic:

specific product
→ produced in Kazakhstan
→ using Kazakhstan-specific advantages
→ sold to identifiable domestic or foreign customers.

---

# 3. Scope is intentionally broad

There are effectively no predefined restrictions on:

- sectors;
- subsectors;
- geography within Kazakhstan;
- domestic vs export orientation;
- size of the opportunity;
- CAPEX;
- investment horizon;
- analytical methodology.

Potential niches may include:

- mining and metals;
- manufacturing;
- agriculture / agri-food;
- infrastructure;
- digital industries;
- services;
- IT services;
- other sectors.

Note:
An unclear term early in the transcript sounded like "ДНК / ДМК", but based on repeated later references, this most likely referred to **ГМК**, the mining and metallurgical complex.

This interpretation should not be treated as an official additional requirement.

---

# 4. A niche can be broad or extremely narrow

KPMG deliberately does not prescribe the granularity of an investment niche.

A niche may be:

### Broad
Example mentioned:
a large glass cluster covering multiple products that can be manufactured from glass.

### Narrow
Example mentioned:
a highly specific product, potentially even defined down to an 8-digit HS / TN VED product code.

Therefore:

sector ≠ necessarily investment niche.

The team may choose the appropriate level of granularity depending on where the strongest investment thesis exists.

---

# 5. There is no single correct methodology

KPMG explicitly described the project as creative.

There is:

- no single correct answer;
- no mandatory framework;
- no mandatory analytical method.

Judges will pay particular attention to:

- the logic of the approach;
- how opportunities were identified;
- how opportunities were filtered;
- how the shortlist was created;
- why the final five were selected.

The methodology should therefore support judgment rather than replace it.

---

# 6. Longlist and shortlist expectations

KPMG wants to see the team's opportunity discovery process.

The deliverable should show:

1. initial hypotheses / opportunity universe;
2. longlist;
3. shortlist;
4. logic / methodology used to narrow opportunities;
5. eventual selection of five investment niches.

Important oral guidance:

Teams should not assume that a shortlist must contain only 10 or 15 opportunities.

Examples from actual KPMG work mentioned during the briefing included shortlists of approximately:

- 37 opportunities;
- 82 opportunities.

The exact number is not important.

What matters is the logic and quality of the approach.

Implication:
The team should search broadly before converging on the final five opportunities.

---

# 7. Top five opportunities

The final analysis must identify exactly five investment niches.

For these five opportunities, KPMG expects the team to show the complete analytical logic supporting their selection, using:

- statistics;
- market evidence;
- investment analysis;
- assumptions where necessary;
- strategic reasoning;
- feasibility considerations.

The five should not simply be the five highest outputs of a mechanical scoring model.

The team needs to be able to explain why each opportunity deserves to be in the final set.

---

# 8. Investor economics

A key principle emphasized in the briefing:

> The economics must work for the investor.

An opportunity should therefore answer questions such as:

- Who pays?
- What market is being served?
- Is demand large enough?
- What makes the opportunity commercially attractive?
- Can the project reach sufficient scale?
- Is Kazakhstan a competitive production location?
- Why would a strategic investor allocate capital here?

Government or national strategic benefits alone are insufficient.

---

# 9. Domestic market size can be a constraint

KPMG highlighted that Kazakhstan's domestic market may be too small for some large foreign investors.

Example discussed:
certain types of cable / wire manufacturing.

If the realistic addressable market is almost exclusively Kazakhstan, the opportunity may be too small to attract a major strategic investor.

Therefore teams should consider:

- domestic demand;
- neighboring demand;
- export potential;
- regional scale;
- global demand where relevant.

For many industrial opportunities, exportability may be important to achieving sufficient scale.

This is a case-specific judgment, not an absolute rule for every niche.

---

# 10. Why Kazakhstan must beat alternative locations

Teams should evaluate Kazakhstan relative to realistic competing production locations.

KPMG gave light manufacturing / cotton socks as an example of a potentially weak opportunity.

Reasons mentioned included:

- strong Chinese competition;
- Uzbekistan's established cotton and light-industry base;
- regional water constraints.

The implication is that:

available demand + available inputs

does NOT automatically equal

an attractive Kazakhstan investment opportunity.

For every serious niche, teams should ask:

> Why should an investor locate this activity in Kazakhstan rather than in the most credible competing country?

---

# 11. Strategic fit with Kazakhstan matters

KPMG expects opportunities to make sense within Kazakhstan's longer-term strategic development.

Teams should consider whether the proposed niche:

- fits Kazakhstan's economic direction;
- builds on realistic national capabilities;
- contributes to future competitiveness;
- is consistent with resource and infrastructure constraints;
- can plausibly become part of Kazakhstan's economy.

A strategically fashionable industry is not automatically attractive if Kazakhstan lacks the ability to compete.

---

# 12. Technology and capability feasibility

KPMG emphasized that an opportunity can look strategically attractive but still be unrealistic.

Example discussion:
data centers and related equipment / components.

The representative's broader point was that teams must consider:

- technological complexity;
- required know-how;
- availability of technology;
- likelihood that a foreign investor would transfer technology;
- Kazakhstan's current capabilities;
- speed at which capabilities could realistically be developed;
- international cost competitiveness;
- competition from China and other established producers.

Therefore:

"Kazakhstan currently imports this product"

does NOT automatically imply

"manufacturing this product locally is an attractive FDI opportunity."

Import substitution alone is not a sufficient investment thesis.

---

# 13. Data centers were mentioned as a plausible area to examine

The representative suggested that infrastructure-related opportunities may emerge during the analysis and specifically mentioned data centers as an example given Kazakhstan's level of digitalization.

However:

this was an example, not a recommendation from KPMG.

Teams should independently test whether any data-center-related niche is actually attractive.

A partially unclear passage mentioned batteries, wires/cables, and high-voltage equipment in connection with the data-center example.

The exact intended relationship between these products and data centers is ambiguous in the raw transcript.

Do not treat that specific connection as a confirmed KPMG requirement.

---

# 14. Critical minerals and incomplete information

Critical minerals were mentioned as an example of an area where relevant resource data may not be publicly available.

KPMG explicitly permits reasonable assumptions when reliable public data is unavailable.

However assumptions must be:

- clearly identified;
- justified;
- based on evidence where possible;
- accompanied by sources explaining the basis of the assumption.

The absence of perfect data does not prevent analysis.

Unsupported invented numbers do.

---

# 15. Sources are mandatory

This was one of the strongest oral instructions.

All deliverables should contain sources.

This includes:

- presentations;
- Excel files;
- analytical materials;
- investment teaser;
- supporting calculations.

Sources should include working links.

The representative stated that materials without sources may simply be skipped during review.

Therefore source tracking must happen during research, not after analysis is completed.

For important claims, preserve:

- claim;
- source title;
- organization;
- working URL;
- date / year where relevant;
- calculation or interpretation based on the source.

---

# 16. Originality and non-obvious opportunities

KPMG expects obvious sectors to appear during initial screening.

Examples mentioned included:

- mining and metals;
- copper;
- aluminium;
- infrastructure;
- other conventional industrial opportunities.

However, teams should also search for less obvious niches.

The representative specifically encouraged teams to look for "know-how" / non-obvious opportunities.

Important example from previous KPMG work:

KPMG previously identified an unexpected investment niche that was not discovered simply through statistical screening.

The idea emerged from combining:

- broader trends;
- Kazakhstan's position;
- neighboring-country conditions;
- other structural factors.

According to the representative, one particularly interesting niche identified in previous work was eventually implemented.

The exact niche was not disclosed.

Key implication:

A strong discovery process should contain both:

### Data-driven discovery
trade data, markets, resources, imports, exports, growth, etc.

and

### Thesis-driven discovery
global trends × Kazakhstan capabilities × neighboring markets × structural change.

Do not rely exclusively on a mechanical statistical screen.

---

# 17. Practicality and feasibility

Teams should test whether opportunities can realistically be implemented.

Relevant questions include:

- Is the technology accessible?
- Are inputs available?
- Is infrastructure adequate?
- Is sufficient demand accessible?
- Can Kazakhstan compete internationally?
- Is the investment scale realistic?
- Are there major resource constraints?
- What key enablers would be required?
- What are the main execution risks?

A creative opportunity that cannot plausibly be executed is weak.

---

# 18. Existing competition is allowed

An investment niche does not need to be a completely new market.

The representative indicated that:

- competition is acceptable;
- monopoly is not necessarily desirable;
- in many cases several players may be healthier strategically;
- teams should avoid proposing FDI that simply displaces or destroys existing domestic producers without a strong justification.

Therefore the objective is not:

"find a market where an investor can become a monopoly."

The objective is:

"find a market where additional investment creates a sustainable and attractive economic opportunity."

---

# 19. Projection horizon

When asked whether projections should cover 3, 5, or 7 years, the representative indicated:

- the appropriate horizon depends on the type of production / project;
- approximately five years is a reasonable general reference.

Therefore:

5 years can be used as a default where appropriate, but it is not a rigid requirement.

Infrastructure or long-ramp projects may require a different justified horizon.

---

# 20. Investor teaser

Only one of the final five niches needs a teaser.

The teaser should be no more than three pages/slides.

The representative described the intended investor reaction roughly as:

> "I understand the economics.
> I understand the market.
> I understand the location.
> I understand the opportunity."

The teaser should therefore be designed from the investor's perspective.

After reading it, the investor should have very few fundamental unanswered questions.

Likely information includes:

- what is being built / developed;
- where;
- target market;
- demand;
- why Kazakhstan;
- investment size;
- economics / investment logic;
- expected impact;
- major risks / enablers.

The teaser is not simply a summary of the team's research.

It is an investor-facing proposition.

---

# 21. Potential investor list

During Q&A, KPMG indicated that identifying a pool of potential investors is not an explicit mandatory deliverable.

However, a credible view of potential investor types or companies may strengthen the analysis where useful.

Treat this as:

OPTIONAL ENHANCEMENT

not:

MANDATORY REQUIREMENT.

---

# 22. AI use and quality expectations

The representative warned that obviously AI-generated work would be viewed negatively and might be skipped.

The briefing did not provide a formal written AI policy in the materials reviewed so far.

Therefore this comment should NOT be interpreted as a confirmed ban on AI.

It does indicate a strong quality expectation:

Avoid:

- generic AI prose;
- unsupported claims;
- generic frameworks;
- shallow lists;
- artificial precision;
- repetitive consulting language;
- obvious copy-paste outputs.

AI should support:

- research;
- source discovery;
- data processing;
- calculations;
- hypothesis generation;
- alternative generation;
- validation;
- critique.

Human team members should own:

- creative connections;
- opportunity selection;
- strategic judgment;
- trade-offs;
- final recommendations;
- storytelling.

If a formal AI policy is later issued, it overrides this working interpretation.

---

# 23. Teamwork

Team collaboration will matter, particularly for teams reaching later stages / finals.

KPMG emphasized consulting as a team activity.

They may observe:

- how team members collaborate;
- whether they support each other;
- consistency across workstreams;
- professionalism.

The five niche analyses should therefore feel like one coherent team output rather than five unrelated mini-projects.

---

# 24. Practical interpretation of a strong niche

Based on the oral briefing, a strong opportunity should be able to answer:

1. WHAT exactly is the investment opportunity?
2. WHO buys the product or service?
3. HOW LARGE is the accessible market?
4. WHY NOW?
5. WHY KAZAKHSTAN?
6. WHY Kazakhstan rather than a credible competing location?
7. WHAT advantage makes the economics attractive?
8. CAN Kazakhstan realistically execute it?
9. CAN the opportunity reach sufficient scale?
10. WHAT strategic value does it create for Kazakhstan?
11. WHAT could make the thesis fail?
12. WHY would a real strategic investor allocate capital to it?

These questions are a team interpretation of the briefing, not an official KPMG scoring framework.

---

# 25. Working implications for opportunity discovery

Do not immediately search for "the five best industries in Kazakhstan."

Instead:

1. build a broad opportunity universe;
2. include both obvious and non-obvious hypotheses;
3. preserve the discovery logic behind each hypothesis;
4. remove opportunities that fail fundamental economic or feasibility tests;
5. compare Kazakhstan against credible alternative locations;
6. investigate promising survivors more deeply;
7. use both quantitative screening and human strategic judgment;
8. only then converge toward the final five.

The exact screening methodology has NOT yet been decided by the team.

# Bottom line

KPMG is not primarily testing whether teams can list attractive industries.

They are testing whether the team can:

broadly discover opportunities
→ identify non-obvious hypotheses
→ test real demand
→ understand Kazakhstan's competitive position
→ evaluate investor economics
→ filter out unrealistic ideas
→ select five defensible niches
→ explain the evidence and assumptions
→ package one opportunity into a credible investor proposition.

The central standard is:

**Would a real strategic investor plausibly put capital into this opportunity in Kazakhstan, and can we prove why?**
